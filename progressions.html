The capo. The cards show the shapes your fingers make. The capo changes what those shapes sound like, not what they look like. G shapes, capo on 2, and the room hears A. The ruler under the cards shows every fret and what you would sound like there. Tap one.

The small numerals above each chord (I, IV, V, vi) are its job in the key: I is home, V pulls you back home, lowercase means minor. You do not need them yet. They are there so that one day you notice every song uses the same handful of moves.

What your hands can do

A barre chord is one where your index finger lies flat across all six strings, like F. If yours still buzz, pick open chords only. This rates the moves between chords, not just the shapes, because that is where beginners get stuck.

Shapes you play

The five keys a guitar is easy in. Pick the one whose chords you know. Every card above is drawn in these shapes; the capo decides what they sound like.

Capo

A capo is a clamp that pins all six strings at one fret. Each fret it moves up raises everything a half step. G shapes with the capo on 2 sound like A; on 4 they sound like B. Use the ruler above to move it. Say no here and the ruler locks to none.

Under your fingers

The change is
the hard part.

Diagrams are the neck as you would see it held upright in front of you: thickest string on the left, nut at the top. Numbers in the dots are fingers (1 index, 2 middle, 3 ring, 4 pinky). A hollow circle above a string means play it open. An × means leave that string out. A thick bar is your index finger flattened across several strings. Numbers under each card are the frets, thick string to thin. Forty more shapes on Play Along.

Strum

One box per half-beat, counted 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &. Your strumming hand swings down on the numbers and up on the "ands" the whole time. The pattern only decides which swings touch the strings.

Capo calculator

Same key,
easier shapes.

First the rule in the direction you use it: shapes plus capo equals key. Then the rule backwards: you want a key, here is where to put the capo.

What a capo does. It is a clamp that presses all six strings down at one fret, so the guitar behaves as if that fret were the nut. Every shape you already know now starts from there. Your fingers make the same G, but the whole instrument has moved up.

The rule. One fret is one half step. Count the frets, count the half steps. G shapes with the capo on fret 2 are two half steps up from G, which is A. On fret 4, four half steps, which is B. On fret 3 it is B♭, on fret 5 it is C. The ruler in the machine above does the counting for you; this is all it is doing.

Why bother. Guitars are easy in C, G, D, A and E because those keys use open strings. Singers do not care about that, and plenty of songs live in B♭, E♭, F or A♭. Without a capo those keys mean barre chords the whole way through. With one, they mean the five shapes you learned first, moved up.

The table below runs the rule backwards. You want the room to hear one particular key. There are several places to put the capo that all get there, each with a different set of shapes. Every row sounds identical to a listener; they differ only in how hard the changes are for your hands. Clamp just behind the fret wire, not on top of it, and only as tight as it needs to be. Over-clamping pulls everything sharp.

Structure

A progression
is not a song yet.

Four chords in a loop is a foundation. Here is a shape to hang it on. The chorus is built out of your verse, not pulled out of a hat.

A verse is where the story goes and it repeats with different words. The chorus is the part everyone sings, same words every time, usually higher. A pre-chorus is the short ramp between them. A bridge shows up once, late, to break the pattern before the last chorus. Verse, verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus is a whole song.

Parts for the room

Four people,
four things to play.

When six guitars all strum full chords it turns to mud. Hand these out and the same progression starts sounding arranged.

Two words that come up below. The root is the note a chord is named after: the G in a G chord. It is the lowest note in every open shape on this page. Pentatonic means five notes, chosen so that none of them clash with any chord in the key. That is why a beginner can wander around the box below and nothing sounds wrong.

First things first

Tune it.

Uses the microphone in this browser only. Nothing is recorded and nothing leaves the page. Play one string at a time and let it ring. The six strings, thickest to thinnest, are E A D G B e. Flat means too low, turn the peg to tighten. Sharp means too high, loosen it. If you overshoot, go a little below the note and come back up; approaching from below is what makes it stay in tune.
