Press Play. You will hear four clicks, then the chords. The one playing lifts and turns teal. Each chord gets one bar (four beats) before the next.

Play along. The diagrams further down show where your fingers go. Do not worry about the strum yet. Hit each chord once when it changes and you are already playing the song.

Like a chord? Lock it. The padlock on each card keeps that chord. Spin the unlocked swaps the others for something else real songs use.

The capo. The cards show the chords your fingers make. The capo changes what those chords sound like, not what they look like. G-family chords with the capo on fret 2, and the room hears A. The ruler under the cards shows every fret and what you would sound like there. Tap one.

Too hard? Say so. Set What your hands can do to open chords only and the tool will never hand you a barre chord. If a change still feels impossible, press Same sound, easier hands.

The small numerals above each chord (I, IV, V, vi) are its job in the key: I is home, V pulls you back home, lowercase means minor. You do not need them yet. They are there so that one day you notice every song uses the same handful of moves.

Make it sound in…

What these controls mean

Chords you know. Songs are built from a small family of chords that belong together, named after the one that feels like home. Most people start with G or D because neither needs a barre chord. The C family has F in it, and F is a rite of passage, not a beginning.

Your hands. A barre chord is one where your index finger lies flat across all six strings, like F. This setting rates the moves between chords, not just the shapes, because the move is where beginners get stuck. A barre you never have to jump to is easier than a C to F.

Capo. A clamp that pins all six strings at one fret. Each fret it moves up raises everything a half step, so the G family with the capo on fret 2 sounds like A and on fret 4 like B. Say you do not have one and the ruler locks to none.

Feel. Each of these points at a different harmonic trick, not just major versus minor.

Style. Style mostly lives in the strum. The standard pattern has downstrokes and upstrokes in it and fits nearly everything; every other style changes the pattern and the tempo.

Make it sound in. The rule backwards. You name the key you want the room to hear, the tool picks the chord family and capo fret that get there with the easiest changes, and tells you what it chose.

Under your fingers

The change is
the hard part.

Diagrams are the neck held upright in front of you: thickest string on the left, nut at the top. Numbers in the dots are fingers (1 index, 2 middle, 3 ring, 4 pinky). A hollow circle above a string means play it open, an × means leave it out, a thick bar is your index finger flattened across several strings. Numbers under each card are frets, thick string to thin. Forty more shapes on Play Along.

Strum

One box per half-beat, counted 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &. Your strumming hand swings down on the numbers and up on the "ands" the whole time. The pattern only decides which swings touch the strings.

Capo calculator

Same key,
easier shapes.

First the rule in the direction you use it: chords plus capo equals key. Then the rule backwards: you want a key, here is where to put the capo.

What a capo does. It is a clamp that presses all six strings down at one fret, so the guitar behaves as if that fret were the nut. Every chord you already know now starts from there. Your fingers make the same G, but the whole instrument has moved up.

The rule. One fret is one half step. Count the frets, count the half steps. G-family chords with the capo on fret 2 are two half steps up from G, which is A. On fret 4, four half steps, which is B. On fret 3 it is B♭, on fret 5 it is C. The ruler in the machine above does the counting for you; this is all it is doing.

Why bother. Guitars are easy in C, G, D, A and E because those keys use open strings. Singers do not care about that, and plenty of songs live in B♭, E♭, F or A♭. Without a capo those keys mean barre chords the whole way through. With one, they mean the chords you learned first, moved up.

The table runs the rule backwards. You want the room to hear one particular key. There are several places to put the capo that all get there, each with a different chord family. Every row sounds identical to a listener; they differ only in how hard the changes are for your hands. Clamp just behind the fret wire, not on top of it, and only as tight as it needs to be. Over-clamping pulls everything sharp.
